From sounding rockets to the Moon

The programme grew in deliberate steps: first rockets, then satellites, then the vehicles to launch them, then deep space.

Building capability one step at a time

Each step was built on the one before: rockets, then satellites, then launchers, then deep space.

India's first rocket launch, a small sounding rocket, lifted off from Thumba near Thiruvananthapuram in November 1963. The site was chosen because it lies close to the magnetic equator, which made it valuable for upper-atmosphere research.

The first Indian satellite, Aryabhata, followed in 1975 on a Soviet launcher. Five years later the SLV-3 rocket placed the Rohini satellite in orbit, making India a nation able to launch its own satellites.

The decades since have been spent on reliable launchers and application satellites for communications, weather and Earth observation, before turning outward to the Moon, Mars and the Sun.

How India launches

Three operational rockets cover most national needs; the heaviest also carries the human spaceflight programme.

The three operational launch vehicles

PSLV

The workhorse

  • Optimised for polar and sun-synchronous orbits
  • Carried Chandrayaan-1 and the Mars Orbiter Mission
  • Long record of successful flights

GSLV Mk II

Indigenous cryogenic stage

  • Places communication and navigation satellites in transfer orbit
  • Uses an Indian-built cryogenic upper stage
  • Medium-lift role

LVM3

Heavy lift

  • Launched Chandrayaan-2 and Chandrayaan-3
  • Flies commercial constellation batches
  • A human-rated version is planned for Gaganyaan

Assessment

The programme's strength is steady, low-cost progress: each mission reuses proven hardware and adds one new capability. The main risks to the next phase are schedule, since human spaceflight carries far stricter safety demands, and the pace at which industry can take over routine launches.
